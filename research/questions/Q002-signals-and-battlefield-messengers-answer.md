# Q002 — Signals and battlefield messengers: M0 answer

**Status:** Answered, 22 November 2026  
**Needed for:** M0 order/signalling system  
**Decision informed:** A short historically defensible signal menu and personnel capable of carrying verbal orders/reports. **No historical timings, hearing radii or message-loss rates are claimed.**

**Already in Notion:** Workstream 03 and the Research Hub already establish imperfect perception, orders decided/sent/received/understood/executed, Goldsworthy's treatment of standards, signals and dispatch riders, the Gergovia audible-recall failure, and the Polybian and Scipionic cases of prearranged conditional action. What M0 still needs is a **minimal bounded vocabulary** and **carrier list**, not another communications theory.

## Signal meanings defensible for the setting

| Meaning available in M0 | Evidence and necessary limit | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Recall / disengage / return to one's line** | Livy 30.34.11 specifically says Scipio ordered the **recall sounded for the hastati** at Zama (202 BCE), using *receptui ... canere*. Livy 25.19 also reports **both armies** receiving a *signum receptui* in a battle near Capua (212 BCE). These report a meaning, but neither proves uniform acoustic range or identical practice in every army. | **L30: read in part; L25: read in part; direct reported meaning.** |
| **Cavalry charge / engage now** | Livy 25.19 reports a signal given to mounted troops to ride against the enemy (*signum equitibus datum ... admitterent equos*). The **meaning** is recorded; the physical signal medium is not specified. Treat as a prearranged unit/wing-specific cue, not a universally recognised trumpet tune. | **L25: read in part; direct reported meaning.** |
| **Execute a predetermined ambush / reserve contingency** | Polybius 3.105 describes a signal being given to Hannibal's hidden force against Minucius (217 BCE), immediately followed by its emergence and attack. Polybius does **not** specify the instrument, sound, line of sight or code. Encode only if the plan already specifies what the cue means. | **P3: read in part; reported contingency signal, medium unknown.** |
| **Assembly and departure stages, outside combat** | Polybius 6.40 describes three successive signals for preparing to leave camp, loading baggage and beginning to march. Polybius 6.36 describes the watch-trumpet; 14.3 says trumpeters/buglers signalled the evening watch routine at Scipio's camp. These prove multiple conventional military signals **outside immediate tactical combat**, not a battlefield menu for flank refusal or reserve commitment. | **P6/P14: read in part; direct non-battlefield meanings.** |

**M0 minimum:** a **specific recall cue** and a **generic prearranged action cue** (charge, ambush or commit reserve only when defined before battle). An **advance/engage** cue can be used as an *engineering extension* to the attested cavalry-charge example, but do **not** label an all-army standardised “advance,” “extend flank,” “support left” or “reserve goes in” acoustic code as proven for 220–100 BCE. Standards provide location, identity and leadership cues; the passages checked do not establish a rich alphabet of Roman or Carthaginian flag orders. The type and reach of Punic signalling are particularly underdocumented: Polybius reports Hannibal's contingency signal but not a standard Punic trumpet vocabulary.

## Who carried messages and how could transmission fail?

| Reported or defensible carrier | Concrete evidence / limit | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Commander's attendants / aides-de-camp** | Before Ilipa (206 BCE), Scipio sent **aides-de-camp** to instruct tribunes and soldiers to eat, arm and march (Polybius 11.22.4). This is a specifically attested bearer class, albeit for orders before deployment rather than a courier racing under fire. | **P11: read in part; directly reported.** |
| **Military tribunes, centurions and cavalry officers conveying instructions down the command chain** | Polybius 6.40 explains the consul→tribunes→centurions/cavalry leaders→troops chain for orders around camp/march. These officers transmit and interpret orders by role; they are not all dedicated mounted couriers. | **P6: read in part; direct institutional description, not a timed battlefield delivery.** |
| **Delegated wing/contingent commanders** | At the African camps Scipio told Laelius and Masinissa their assignments personally beforehand (Polybius 14.4); they could then execute contingent instructions without a new tactical message. This is **advance delegation**, not proof of instant wing-to-headquarters communication. | **P14: read in part; direct order to officers.** |
| **Mounted or foot messengers / runners** | Either is a physically plausible implementation for between-formation messages, but **the checked 220–100 BCE passages do not document a universal mandated class, mounted delivery norm, speed or survival rate**. Mark these as **engineering carrier options**. | **Engineering assumption; no period-specific rate/source claim.** |

**Failures and adverse controls:** Livy's account of Zama establishes an order to sound the recall but does not tell us who heard it. Polybius' Raphia account (5.85, 217 BCE) shows a senior officer warning Antiochus that the wider battle had turned during his mounted pursuit; he attempted to return **too late**. This is a late situational warning, not necessarily a courier intercepted or disbelieved. Caesar *BG* 7.47–52 describes a trumpet recall not heard across a valley at Gergovia (52 BCE): useful **later comparative evidence**, explicitly **outside** the requested period; Caesar also had reputational reasons to blame troops for continuing. **No verified 220–100 BCE instance checked establishes a particular signal physically heard by the wrong unit, a named battlefield messenger killed in transit, or a messenger-disbelief rate.** Preserve the failure possibilities as testable assumptions, not claims of documented frequency.
