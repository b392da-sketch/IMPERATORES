# Q004 — Cavalry pursuit, return and rally: M0 answer

**Status:** Answered, 22 November 2026  
**Needed for:** M0 pursuit/rally decisions  
**Decision informed:** Historically plausible options for a mounted commander after an opponent withdraws or routs: pursue, halt, redirect to infantry, return to battle, or attempt to rally.

**Already in Notion:** Workstream 03 covers Hasdrubal's switch from pursuit to infantry-rear attack at Cannae and Laelius/Masinissa's return at Zama; Goldsworthy's cavalry/pursuit material and the retreat/rout workstream already warn against treating all flight as identical. The remaining design-facing need is a compact comparison of cases, **not pursuit probabilities**.

| Case (date) | Observed outcome of cavalry movement | Reason actually stated; limits and hindsight | Ancient text and access grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Ticinus (218 BCE), Roman mounted defeat** | Hannibal's heavier horse attacked head-on while Numidians outflanked and struck the Roman rear; Roman cavalry fled or withdrew with the wounded consul. | Polybius attributes the result to the combination of close combat and flank pressure. **No explicit choice between prolonged pursuit versus return to infantry** is documented in this phase. | **P3, 3.65: read in part**; Polybius' tactical explanation is retrospective. |
| **2. Scipio's withdrawal after Ticinus (218)** | Hannibal dispatched Numidian and other mounted forces after a withdrawing Roman army; Numidians instead paused to burn its abandoned camp, allowing the Romans to get farther away. | **Distraction by burning/plundering the camp** is stated in Polybius. His claim that the Romans would otherwise have suffered heavily is a historian's counterfactual, not a measured result. | **P3, 3.68: read in part**. |
| **3. Trebia (218), mounted success and pursuit** | The Punic cavalry beat Roman horse on the wings and added pressure on Roman infantry alongside the Numidians; Roman cavalry and a substantial body of infantry ultimately escaped to Placentia. | Polybius stresses Punic mounted superiority and Roman fatigue/cold; **specific horse-unit pursuit duration or deliberate regroup command is not given**. | **P3, 3.72–74: read in part**. Reported strengths, not measured. |
| **4. Raphia (217), Antiochus' victorious right wing** | Antiochus routed opposing cavalry, pursued too far, and tried to gallop back with royal horse after a senior officer warned him that the central battle was being lost; he arrived too late. | Polybius attributes overpursuit partly to Antiochus' youthful confidence; this is **literary praise/blame and hindsight**, not a directly observed psychological measurement. A delayed warning/return opportunity is explicit. | **P5, 5.84–85: read in part**. Hellenistic comparator, not a Hannibalic unit template. |
| **5. Raphia (217), Echecrates' other wing** | Echecrates steered cavalry around the elephant confrontation, attacked opposing mounted troops in flank/rear and routed them while directing nearby infantry action. | Polybius explicitly says he reacted to observed dust/elephants and adjusted the approach. The narrative **does not quantify how long he chased horsemen** or establish a standard return timetable. | **P5, 5.85: read in part**. |
| **6. Cannae (216), Hasdrubal's cavalry** | After mounted victories, Hasdrubal left pursuit to Numidians and redirected other cavalry against Roman infantry rear, helping complete the defeat. | Polybius explicitly praises Hasdrubal's choice of **task allocation**; prior orders from Hannibal are unrecorded, so independence cannot be proven absolutely. | **P3, 3.115–116: read in part**. |
| **7. Ilipa's preliminary mounted encounter (206)** | Mago/Masinissa's Carthaginian and Numidian horse first retired in reasonably good order; Roman pressure eventually broke the squadrons and drove them under their own camp. | Polybius stresses surprise and Roman pressure. **This is temporary ordered retirement followed by mounted rout**, not proof they reformed and returned to the *same* engagement; their cavalry skirmished again on later days. | **P11, 11.21: read in part**. |
| **8. Zama (202), Laelius and Masinissa** | Roman-allied cavalry defeated enemy horse, pursued off the infantry field, then returned in time to attack Hannibal's rear. | The return is expressly reported but **the source does not document a precise Scipionic recall order, cavalry travel time or predetermined return schedule**. Fortuitous literary timing must remain possible. | **P15, 15.12–14: read in part**; Livy 30.35 partly derivative, not independently counted. |
| **9. Near Capua (212), opposing cavalry disengage** | After sharp cavalry combat, the appearance of a distant force alarmed **both sides**; both were given recall signals and withdrew to camp. | Livy reports **fear of unfamiliar arriving reinforcements** and signals; no cavalry rout or extended chase. A good **stop/recall** alternative. | **L25, 25.19: read in part**, later Roman narration. |
| **10. Magnesia (190), Antiochus and Roman/allied troops** | Antiochus drove the Roman left back toward camp and followed; a Roman camp officer compelled fleeing men to halt and turn back, while Attalus arrived with **200 cavalry (Livy-reported)** to help resist Antiochus. | The reported rallied fugitives are **not securely identified as a routed cavalry formation returning independently**—some were likely infantry; do not silently transform this into an unambiguous mounted rally case. Livy supplies camp safety, coercion and local reinforcements as mechanisms. His scene is retrospective and dramatic. | **L37, 37.42–43: read in part**. |

**Options supported for M0:** Continue mounted pursuit; break off/stop when threat or recall becomes apparent; redirect victorious cavalry to a more decisive flank/rear attack; return from pursuit to the main battlefield; conduct a staged withdrawal under pressure; attempt to rally fugitives where friendly support/camp/other troops are available. The last option has **weaker cavalry-specific direct support** in the checked sample than the others.

**Influences to vary, not historical rates:** existing orders and discretion; local view of friendly infantry; enemy horse's condition; pursuit distance; terrain, camp and obstacles; horses' fatigue; losses/cohesion; potential plunder; opportunity for a flank attack; perceived arrival of reinforcements; receipt of a warning/recall. Do not turn Polybius' comments on Antiochus' immaturity into a universal tendency or give Numidians a fixed ethnic pursuit bonus.

**Important negative finding:** Within the checked 220–100 BCE sample, I did **not** verify an unequivocal case of a **routed cavalry formation recovering and returning to decide that very same battle**. Livy 37.43 concerns fugitives rallying near camp, but their mounted/infantry composition is insufficiently specified. Treat a mounted **rout→rally→return** transition as **an experimental option**, not a documented historical frequency. Victorious cavalry returning from pursuit at Zama is a different behaviour and is directly attested.
